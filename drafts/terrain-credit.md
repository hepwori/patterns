## Context

Organizations differ in how settled their problem space is. Four properties tend to travel together:

- **A common currency.** Every project can be measured in the same unit, often money.
- **A nameable customer.** Someone can be identified, found, and asked.
- **A known causal chain.** Effort put in reliably maps to outcomes that can be observed.
- **A short horizon.** The time between acting and knowing is measured in quarters, not years.

Where these hold, the work is *converged*. Roadmaps cohere, priorities can be argued with evidence, and delivery is predictable. Where they don't, the work is *divergent*: the organization is exploring, and no one, inside or outside, yet knows which metrics matter or how they play out.

Convergence can be earned, and it can be imported. An industry analyst's quadrant, a competitor's feature set, or an established set of buying metrics all hand an organization the answers to questions someone else already paid to explore. The organization that reads from such a key looks coherent. The organization that has no key must produce one, and producing one looks, from the outside, like a mess.

Both kinds of organization are usually evaluated together, by the same people, using the same instruments.

## Problem

Role profiles, performance ladders, calibration processes, and leadership reviews are written once and applied broadly. They are shaped by the work that is easiest to see and compare: prioritizing against a known metric, holding a roadmap, shipping on schedule. That work is mostly converged work.

Applied across both regimes, the yardstick measures the terrain and reports it as performance. Converged organizations are credited with coherence they did not entirely produce. Divergent organizations are blamed for incoherence that is what exploration looks like.

The error operates at every scale. A leader of a divergent organization looks weaker than a leader of a converged one. A product manager doing the work a divergent space requires (sensing, choosing bets, making sense of ambiguous signal, ending things well) reads as underperforming against a profile calibrated to converged work. A newcomer from a converged organization sees a mess and concludes the people are the problem.

The counterfactual is rarely tested. Nobody swaps the leaders and watches what happens, so the attribution is never falsified.

## Forces

- **Visible coherence, invisible cause:** a tidy roadmap is observable; the reason it was possible is not.
- **One yardstick for fairness:** shared ladders and calibration make evaluation comparable and defensible, and they flatten differences in terrain in the process.
- **Attribution to people:** outcomes are explained by who was in charge more readily than by the conditions they worked in.
- **Imported convergence:** because someone else paid for the exploration, the dependence is easy to forget.
- **The newcomer's first read:** outsiders see divergence at full contrast and have not yet done the work that would explain it.
- **Real performance problems exist too:** divergent organizations also contain weak leaders and weak practitioners, and terrain provides cover for them. The pattern explains a systematic bias; it does not excuse any particular case.

## Navigation Strategies

Name the terrain before the comparison is made. At planning time, before a review cycle, and when someone moves between organizations, say which regime the work is in and what the missing properties are.

**Describe excellence per regime.** A role profile can state what strong looks like when the space is settled and what it looks like when it isn't, and calibrate people within their regime first.

**Ask for divergent-appropriate evidence.** In an unsettled space, delivery predictability is weak evidence. Rate of learning, quality of bets chosen and abandoned, time taken to end a failing direction, and the soundness of decisions made under uncertainty are stronger.

**Compare each organization with its own past and with peers in the same regime**, not with the most legible organization nearby.

**Treat the newcomer's first read as information about the yardstick.** Give a deliberate window of doing the work before conclusions harden. Newcomers who revise their view tend to do so after meeting the missing answer key firsthand.

**Run the swap test in the imagination.** Before crediting or blaming a leader, ask whether their results would hold in the other terrain. If no one has ever crossed over, treat the attribution as unproven.

**Check where convergence came from.** If an organization's coherence rests on an answer key someone else produced, say so.

## Consequences

- Credit and blame accumulate in the wrong places. Converged organizations learn that their method is the reason they succeed and export it to places it doesn't fit.
- Divergent organizations are pressured to adopt the converged organization's artifacts: roadmaps with dates, ROI models, single success metrics. They comply with the appearance, which feeds [[Fluency Capture]] and [[Perverse Correlation]].
- Ambitious people route toward the organizations where they will be seen to succeed. The divergent organizations that most need strong judgment, because they have no metric to lean on, receive less of it.
- Practitioners in divergent organizations are rated against a profile their work doesn't fit, which affects promotion and retention independent of their contribution.

### Degeneration: The Revolving Door

Divergent terrain looks chaotic. The chaos is attributed to leadership, and the leader is replaced. The new leader arrives with a new thesis and re-plans. The reset adds to the apparent chaos, which confirms the verdict, and the cycle repeats.

The terrain sets the starting condition. The misattribution compounds it. Over time the organization's incoherence is partly self-inflicted, which makes terrain and leadership hard to separate and keeps the story "we still haven't found the right leader" alive.

## Known Uses

**Twitter, 2014.** Two large product engineering organizations were seen very differently. Revenue was known as high-functioning: predictable, focused, coherent roadmaps, reliable delivery. Consumer was known as a mess: meandering, unpredictable, almost Brownian in its motion.

Revenue worked in digital advertising, a familiar, established domain with a clear north star. Returns on engineering could be measured directly in dollars, large customers could be identified and spoken to, and every project shared a currency. Consumer was building something no one had built before. It was unclear which metrics to optimize, there was no single customer (usage in Japan looked nothing like usage in the United States), and the return on engineering was impossible to calculate.

The difference was credited to leadership: the head of revenue product ran a tight ship, and the head of consumer product looked bad by comparison. Consumer product went through roughly six heads in five years, each with a different theory of what consumer was for.

**A cloud business and an internal platform organization.** A product leader moved from a business that measured everything in revenue to an organization that delivered technology to the rest of the company and had no revenue metric. On arrival, she read the new organization as a mess and the product managers as underperformers, because they were not behaving like the top performers in her previous organization.

Two months in, she revised her view. In the cloud business, she said, "we could just look at the magic quadrant and implement the features checklist known in the market. Over here it is so complicated." The market had already done the exploration there. In the platform organization, no one had produced the key.

## Related Patterns

- [[Absence Accounting]] — a common source of divergence: preventive work has no common currency, so organizations built around it cannot be measured as converged ones are
- [[Fluency Capture]] — the degeneration that follows when a divergent organization adopts the converged organization's artifacts to be judged well
- [[Perverse Correlation]] — what happens to the proxy metrics a divergent organization adopts when it is pressed for a number
- [[Reward Loop Asymmetry]] — the general bias toward what is visible and quickly rewarded, of which a single converged yardstick is one case
- [[Dual Competence]] — practitioners need craft and organizational fluency, and which craft is valued depends on the terrain
