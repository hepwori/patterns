## Context

Organizations differ in how settled their problem space is. Four properties tend
to travel together:

- **A common currency.** Every project can be measured in the same unit, often
  money.
- **A nameable customer.** Someone can be identified, found, and asked.
- **A known causal chain.** Effort put in reliably maps to outcomes that can be
  observed.
- **A short horizon.** The time between acting and knowing is measured in
  quarters, not years.

Where these hold, the work is *convergent*. Roadmaps cohere, priorities can be
argued with evidence, and delivery is predictable. Where they don't, the work is
*divergent*: the organization is exploring, and no one, inside or outside, yet
knows which metrics matter or how they play out.

These are different kinds of work, and the behaviors that lead to success
differ. Convergent work rewards prioritizing against a known measure, holding a
roadmap, and delivering on schedule. Divergent work rewards sensing, choosing
between bets, making sense of ambiguous signal, and ending an experiment early.
A given person can be excellent at one and unremarkable at the other.

Exploration looks chaotic. Plans change, the measures of success are disputed,
and the picture stays incoherent until late. This is not a sign of poor
management. It comes with the territory: an organization that knows where it is
going can be tidy, whereas one still finding out cannot.

The distinction is one of nature, not degree. Work can also vary in difficulty
for other reasons, such as a legacy codebase, a hostile market, or a thin team.
In those cases the same behaviors lead to success; there is just more or less
of a grade to climb. Here the behaviors that succeed are different, and more
effort of the same sort doesn't close the gap.

Convergence depends partly on who has already done the exploring. Some problem
spaces have been explored by others, and the results are shared. Analysts
publish what a complete product looks like, competitors show which features
customers pay for, and the industry settles on standard ways of measuring
success. An organization in such a problem space can build on all of this as a
steady foundation. The effect is profound: its task becomes executing well
against answers that already exist.

An organization operating in uncharted territory has no such answers to build
on. Finding out what matters, for whom, and how to tell is a major part of the
work. A space can move from one condition to the other as exploration succeeds:
an area that was once divergent can become convergent.

A single large company will contain both kinds of work, side by side. The people
doing them have different jobs, even when their titles are the same.

## Problem

The difference between the two kinds of work is hard to see from inside either
one. People who have worked mostly in one kind rarely know there is another, in
the way a fish rarely knows about water. People who have worked in both tend to
carry the model of one into the other. Evaluators lose track of the difference,
practitioners lose track of it, and so do divergent organizations themselves.

In scaled enterprises, work tends to be judged as though it were all convergent.
The instruments of judgment are the role profile, the performance ladder,
calibration, planning reviews, and leadership's sense of what a well-run team
looks like. They are built from work that is easy to see and compare, which is
mostly convergent work. Both kinds of organization are usually evaluated
together, by the same people, using the same instruments.

Misjudgment shows up at three scales:

- **Organizations.** A divergent organization looks undisciplined next to a
  convergent one, and the convergent one is credited with a discipline that owes
  much to its circumstances.
- **Leaders.** The leader of a divergent organization looks weaker than the
  leader of a convergent one, and is more likely to be replaced.
- **Individuals.** A product manager doing what divergent work requires is rated
  against a profile calibrated to convergent work.

Practitioners feel the mismatch before anyone names it. What they do well is
hard for these instruments to see, and what the instruments do see often looks
like weakness.

Stated systematically, the instruments fail in four ways:

- **They cannot see the work.** Much of what matters in divergent work leaves
  little of an artifact trail: a bet declined, an experiment ended early, an
  understanding that formed.
- **They misread the traces.** Revised plans look like indecision. The lack of a
  single metric looks like a lack of focus. Open-ended research looks like a
  lack of progress. The absence of the standard artifacts (strategy, roadmap,
  competitive analysis, segmentation) looks like a lack of rigor.
- **They credit the wrong cause.** A convergent team's coherence is attributed
  to its people, when much of it comes from the kind of work, and sometimes from
  answers produced elsewhere.
- **They place the cause in the person.** When divergent work looks poor,
  evaluators look to the people. The kind of work is rarely considered, and the
  swap that would test it (the same people doing the other kind) is almost never
  run.

**The practitioner's bind.** Someone judged this way has three options. They can
conform: produce the artifacts the instruments reward, such as dated roadmaps,
single success metrics, and ROI models, whether or not the work supports them.
They can absorb: keep doing the work as it needs doing and accept the ratings
and the slower progress. Or they can leave for a convergent organization, where
the same effort is read as strength.

Most also take on a further cost, which is explaining the work: translating it
for evaluators who cannot see it, and defending its legibility. This takes time
away from the product and rarely appears in any evaluation.

## Forces

- **Invisible from inside:** each kind of work feels like simply what work is,
  so the difference goes unremarked until someone crosses between them.
- **Comparison needs a common scale:** ladders and calibration exist to make
  people comparable across teams and evaluation defensible, but they flatten
  differences between kinds of work in the process.
- **Instruments are built from what is visible:** ladders are written from work
  that can be observed and compared, which favors convergent work.
- **Evaluators learned in convergent work:** people who rise through visibly
  successful organizations tend to carry their model of good work with them, and
  they are often the ones doing the evaluating.
- **Visible coherence, invisible cause:** a tidy roadmap can be seen; the reason
  it was possible cannot.
- **Attribution to people:** outcomes are explained by who was in charge more
  readily than by the conditions they worked in.
- **Real performance problems exist too:** divergent organizations also contain
  weak leaders and weak practitioners, and the difficulty of the work gives them
  cover. The pattern explains a systematic bias, not any particular case.

## Navigation Strategies

Name the kind of work before the comparison is made. At planning time, before a
review cycle, and when someone moves between organizations, say which kind the
work is and which of the four properties described in Context (a common
currency, a nameable customer, a known causal chain, a short horizon) are
missing.

For practitioners:

**Make the work legible on its own terms.** Divergent work has its own
equivalents of the standard artifacts: a strawman model of the landscape, kept
explicitly provisional and revised in the open; relationships maintained and
kept informed throughout the exploration; an understanding of the "physics of
the space," meaning how it actually behaves; and the best available account of
how the pieces fit together ("sensemaking"). Some of this lives in heads and
relationships; the rest is sketches and high-level diagrams, which build shared
understanding rather than drive execution or decisions. Making it inspectable
takes deliberate effort. Keep the model versioned, keep a record of bets
considered, chosen, and ended, and be able to say what would change your mind.
This gives evaluators something to read.

**Agree the evidence early.** Settle with evaluators, at the start of a cycle,
what good looks like for this work: rate of learning, quality of bets, time
taken to end an experiment, soundness of decisions under uncertainty.
Where the outcome is alignment, favor evidence of behavior over evidence of
artifacts. Signs of movement include teams using the same language and
analogies, drawing the same inferences, keeping decisions high quality when they
are federated to the edges of the organization, and telling executives a single
story. Shared language and a single story are the easiest to produce without
real understanding. Shared inferences and good decisions at the edge are the
stronger tests.

**Be wary of theater.** Producing the artifacts of convergent work where the
work is divergent earns credit now at the cost of credibility later.

**Find someone who has crossed.** A peer or sponsor who has worked in both kinds
can translate in both directions.

For evaluators and organizations:

**Describe excellence per kind.** A role profile can say what strong looks like
in convergent work and in divergent work, and calibrate people within their kind
first.

**Look for the divergent equivalents.** The absence of standard artifacts is not
evidence of good work, any more than it is evidence of poor work. Ask whether
the practitioner can state a working model of the landscape and what would
change it, who they keep informed, how the space behaves, and how the pieces fit
together. These can be tested: does the model predict how a move will land, and
does it get revised when it is wrong?

**Adjust for degree where the scale is shared.** In diving, judges score
execution and multiply by a published degree of difficulty. Calibration can do
the same where work differs in degree. The four properties from Context make a
usable rubric: count how many are missing. Adjustment is not enough where the
kinds differ.

**Compare each organization with its own past and with peers of the same kind**,
not with the most legible organization nearby.

**Run the swap test in the imagination.** Before crediting or blaming a leader,
ask whether their results would hold in the other kind of work. If no one has
ever crossed over, treat the attribution as unproven.

## Consequences

- Practitioners in divergent organizations are rated against profiles their work
  doesn't fit, which affects promotion and retention independent of their
  contribution.
- Some conform. The organization receives roadmaps and metrics that look like
  those of convergent work and mean less, which feeds [[Fluency Capture]] and
  [[Perverse Correlation]].
- Ambitious people route toward the organizations where they will be seen to
  succeed. The divergent organizations that most need strong judgment, because
  they have no metric to lean on, receive less of it.
- Credit accumulates in the wrong places. Convergent organizations learn that
  their method is the reason they succeed and export it to places it doesn't
  fit.

### Degeneration: The Revolving Door

Divergent work looks chaotic. The chaos is attributed to leadership, and the
leader is replaced. The new leader arrives with a new thesis and re-plans. The
reset adds to the apparent chaos, which confirms the verdict, and the cycle
repeats.

The nature of the work sets the starting condition. The misattribution compounds
it. Over time the organization's incoherence is partly self-inflicted, which
makes the work and the leadership hard to separate and keeps the story "we still
haven't found the right leader" alive.

## Known Uses

**Performance calibration.** A manager took a product manager working in a
divergent area through calibration, where the product manager was compared with
one in a convergent area. The manager described their report as playing on hard
mode, with everything uphill, and won some additional credit that way, but only
some. The framing asked for a handicap on a shared scale. It did not challenge
the scale.

**An internal platform organization and a cloud business.** In an organization
that delivers technology to the rest of its company, product managers voice a
common concern: they are graded against a role profile calibrated to convergent
work. In the sibling cloud business, a product manager moves a revenue metric.
In the platform organization, the nearest equivalent is a notional measure of
alignment, and there is no dashboard for it. A product manager whose primary
outcome is organizational alignment is at a disadvantage next to a peer whose
dashboard shows revenue or adoption.

A product leader who moved from the cloud business to the platform organization
read the new organization, on arrival, as a mess, and its product managers as
underperformers, because they were not behaving like the top performers in her
previous organization. On her cloud team, every product manager kept up-to-date
copies of a standard set of artifacts: strategy, roadmap, PRD, competitive
analysis, market segmentation, SWOT analysis. In the platform organization, none
of her new team had them. At first she was aghast. She read the missing
artifacts as missing rigor.

Two months in, she revised her view. In the cloud business, she said, "we could
just look at the magic quadrant and implement the features checklist known in
the market. Over here it is so complex." The market had already done the
exploration there. In the platform organization, no one had produced the key.

The strongest product managers there had something else: a strawman conceptual
model of the landscape, strong relationships they kept informed during the
exploration, a nuanced understanding of the "physics of the space," and the best
available sensemaking of how the pieces fit together. Some of it lived in heads
and relationships. The rest was sketches and high-level diagrams: artifacts of
gradual sensemaking and organizational alignment, not of execution or
decisions.

**Twitter, 2014.** Two large product engineering organizations were seen very
differently. Revenue was known as high-functioning: predictable, focused,
coherent roadmaps, reliable delivery. Consumer was known as a mess: meandering,
unpredictable, almost Brownian in its motion.

Revenue worked in digital advertising, a familiar, established domain with a
clear north star. Returns on engineering could be measured directly in dollars,
large customers could be identified and spoken to, and every project shared a
currency. Consumer was building something no one had built before. It was
unclear which metrics to optimize, there was no single customer (usage in Japan
looked nothing like usage in the United States), and the return on engineering
was impossible to calculate.

The difference was credited to leadership: the head of revenue product ran a
tight ship, and the head of consumer product looked bad by comparison. Consumer
product went through roughly six heads in five years, each with a different
theory of what consumer was for.

## Related Patterns

- [[Absence Accounting]] — a common source of divergence: preventive work has no
  common currency, so organizations built around it cannot be measured as
  convergent ones are
- [[Fluency Capture]] — the degeneration that follows when a divergent
  organization adopts the artifacts of convergent work to be judged well
- [[Perverse Correlation]] — what happens to the proxy metrics a divergent
  organization adopts when it is pressed for a number
- [[Reward Loop Asymmetry]] — the general bias toward what is visible and
  quickly rewarded, of which a single convergent rubric is one case
- [[Dual Competence]] — practitioners need craft and organizational fluency, and
  which craft is valued depends on the kind of work
- [[Alignment Mirage]] — when the outcome being moved is shared understanding,
  it is also the easiest thing to fake: artifacts can look aligned while the
  understanding diverges
