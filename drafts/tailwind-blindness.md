## Context

Some organizations grow fast for a long stretch. A rising curve is a tailwind: a
force pushing results upward whose source may be external, incidental, or simply
not understood. While the curve rises, the result is easily read as evidence of
the organization's own skill.

## Problem

Nobody audits a rising curve. Inquiry costs attention, and success removes the
felt need for it, so the causal model of what is driving growth never gets
built.

When the curve flattens, the organization needs that model at the moment it is
hardest to build. Nothing was instrumented for the question, the people who
would remember have moved on, and the counterfactuals are gone. "What drove
growth?" becomes archaeology.

The inquiry is cheapest when it feels least necessary.

## Forces

- **Attention cost:** audits cost attention, and a rising curve gives no reason
  to spend it.
- **Success reads as skill:** it is more natural to explain a success by the
  people involved than by circumstance (hypothesis).
- **Memory churn:** the people who would remember what was happening leave or
  move on (hypothesis).
- **No instrumentation in advance:** the data needed to attribute growth is not
  collected until someone knows to ask (hypothesis).
- **Plausible stories are cheap:** when the question finally arrives, a
  confident explanation is easier to produce than a tested one (hypothesis).

## Known Uses

**The growth task force.** A fast-growing company's growth slows. Senior leaders
form a task force, chaired by an executive, that meets a couple of times a week.
After a few meetings the first finding is that nobody knows what drove the
growth in the first place. The company had grown so fast that no one stopped to
ask why; only when the curve flattened did the organization start to reflect.
