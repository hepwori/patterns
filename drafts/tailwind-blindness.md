## Context

Some organizations grow fast for a long stretch. A rising curve is a tailwind: a
force pushing results upward whose source may be external, incidental, or simply
not understood. While the curve rises, the result is easily read as evidence of
the organization's own skill (hypothesis).

Growth of this kind is usually the product of many things at once, some inside
the organization's control and some not, interacting over time (hypothesis).

## Problem

Nobody audits a rising curve. Inquiry costs attention, and success removes the
felt need for it, so the causal model of what is driving growth never gets
built.

When the curve flattens, the organization needs that model at the moment it is
hardest to build. Nothing was instrumented for the question, the people who
would remember have moved on (hypothesis), and the counterfactuals are gone.
"What drove growth?" becomes archaeology.

The inquiry is cheapest when it feels least necessary. The data needed to
attribute growth can only be collected going forward, so every month of
unexamined success is a month of evidence that can no longer be had. The need
arrives only after the window for meeting it has mostly closed.

Late inquiry also tends to return a different kind of answer than the one
hoped for. The organization wants a driver, and what it finds is a tangle: many
causes, none cleanly separable from the others (hypothesis).

## Forces

- **Attention cost:** audits cost attention, and a rising curve gives no reason
  to spend it.
- **Success reads as skill:** it is more natural to explain a success by the
  people involved than by circumstance (hypothesis).
- **Memory churn:** the people who would remember what was happening leave or
  move on (hypothesis).
- **Evidence cannot be backdated:** the instrumentation needed to attribute
  growth only records from the day it is turned on. An organization that never
  built it discovers the gap only when it first needs the answer.
- **Many interacting causes:** growth rarely has a single driver, so even a
  well-resourced late inquiry struggles to separate them (hypothesis).
- **An unexplained gap can be spent:** while no tested model of growth exists,
  any initiative can be pitched as the remedy, and nothing available can rule it
  out (hypothesis).

## Navigation Strategies

Not yet discussed with Isaac. Everything below is Claude's hypothesis.

- **Ask "why is this working?" while it is working.** Put it on a cadence, so
  it does not wait for a reason (hypothesis).
- **Instrument for attribution before it is needed.** Cohorts, holdouts, and
  growth accounting are cheap while the curve rises and impossible to
  reconstruct afterwards (hypothesis).
- **Keep a hypothesis log.** Write competing explanations down while memory is
  fresh, so later work can test them in place of rediscovering them
  (hypothesis).
- **Treat a sustained tailwind as an open question,** not a settled fact
  (hypothesis).
- **Separate the diagnosis from the agenda.** Keep the inquiry into what drove
  growth apart from proposals for what to do about the slowdown (hypothesis).

## Consequences

- The organization cannot tell what to protect or double down on, because it
  does not know what produced the result.
- Strategy gets built on explanations that were never tested.
- The cost of understanding is paid late and all at once. In the use below, the
  inquiry became a standing organization, and a large backlog of missing
  instrumentation and analytics was cleared as a matter of urgency.

### Degeneration: The Deficit as Vehicle

A slowdown with no agreed explanation becomes a resource. Each executive uses
the shortfall to advance an initiative they already favored, framed as the way
to restore growth. Because no tested model exists, none of the initiatives can
be ruled out in favor of another, and the inquiry that would settle the matter
is crowded by the proposals it was supposed to inform (hypothesis).

A post-hoc narrative about what drove growth can also harden into strategy
before it has been tested (hypothesis).

## Known Uses

**The growth task force.** A fast-growing consumer company sees growth slow.
Senior leaders form a task force, chaired by an executive, that meets a couple
of times a week. After a few meetings the first finding is that nobody knows
what drove the growth in the first place. The company had grown so fast that no
one stopped to ask why; only when the curve flattened did the organization start
to reflect.

The task force commissions a great deal of point research. It comes back with
one answer: it's complicated. The task force grows into a full organization
dedicated to studying, understanding, and driving growth. In doing so the
company discovers how much instrumentation and analytics it never built, and
pays that debt down as a matter of urgency.

Meanwhile each executive uses the growth deficit as a vehicle for a favored
initiative.
